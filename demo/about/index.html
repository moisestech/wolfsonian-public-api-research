Archive Dreams in Public · An artist-led research prototype

What if an archive could disagree with itself?

Objects from another century made promises about technology, work, politics, and a better tomorrow. We inherit those promises—but not always the people they spoke for, the people they left out, or the reasons we connect them.

In this interactive experiment, archival records encounter one another through a network. Their dialogue is fictional and artist-authored. The underlying records and the reasons for a connection should remain visible and open to challenge. A compelling interpretation may still be wrong.

Your role is not simply to watch.

  1. Choose an object or connection. Follow a possible relationship through the Atlas.
  2. Hear two positions. Different records confront one another in scripted, interpretive speech.
  3. Meet the Archivist. A consistent fictional voice explains what the available evidence supports—and what it cannot establish.
  4. Decide what deserves further research. Inspect the source, question the link, or follow another object. Some connections are intentionally weak.

The futures we inherited

This research follows the Wolfsonian's interest in the persuasive power of design. World's fairs are a useful entry point: their grand visions of tomorrow were also advertisements, organized journeys, industrial achievements, and selective accounts of society.

The museum's 2025–26 World's Fairs: Visions of Tomorrow exhibition and Marco Brambilla's After Utopia explored those imagined futures through archival material, moving images, and new technologies. This project follows a different formal question: what changes when the visitor can interrupt the narration and inspect the reasons for a proposed connection?

Prior programs: World's Fairs: Visions of Tomorrow · After Utopia. These are research contexts, not a claim of endorsement or direct collaboration with this prototype.

01 · Who narrates?

What this system knows—and does not know

Here, an accession identifies a record; a catalog title describes it; a metadata tag can suggest a comparison. A shared tag is not proof that two objects, makers, or audiences encountered each other. Interpretive dialogue is a contemporary artwork, not an archival quotation, a historical reconstruction of someone's speech, or an official museum statement.

Interpretation is not evidence.

Some nodes use locally supplied presentation photographs; missing images use schematic representations. Lakehouse lookups are a separate data layer and do not overwrite catalog tags. Storage locations stay unknown unless an authorized mapping exists.

This independent prototype is under development. Public exhibition-checklist references do not automatically authorize image reproduction, redistribution of internal metadata, or public use of the museum's collections data.

Recorded dialogue may be played using locally installed device voices. The optional external voice service requires an explicit approval step and receives only displayed words, not the full collection file. Text remains available without audio.

Current state: the 1939 fair and labor records above are research candidates, not new nodes or verified edges in the live Atlas. Any additional scene requires record matching, rights review, source checks, and editorial sign-off.
