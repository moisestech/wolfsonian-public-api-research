# Archive Society conversation review standard

Version 1 · November 23, 2026 · Archive Dreams in Public

Use this standard to review one room before it enters a demo, the app, or audio production. The room starts with a question that only these objects can answer in this way. By the second spoken turn, the visitor should hear a response to that question and a reason these two objects are speaking together.

## Who does what

**Persona**. The recurring fictional identity the visitor chooses, such as Worker. Its name, values, visual mark and phrasing stay recognizable across rooms.

**Archetype**. An editorial grouping for readers with related habits of attention. Everyday readers, Archive & futures, and Critical experiments help organize the picker and the writing. They add no character to a scene.

**Perspective**. The persona’s specific reading of this pair. It determines what the listener notices, what they ask next, and how they phrase it. Selecting it can change the listener text and the graph’s reading without recasting or rewriting the scene.

**Object hosts**. The two objects joined by the edge. Voice follows exact accession across partners and rooms. Write from each object’s purpose, material, title, and documented setting. One object may be confident here and doubtful in another room.

**Archivist**. When selected, the fictional Archivist is a listener. When there is a separately authored Archivist line in the script, it clarifies a specific question of evidence. The line is part of transcript timing. The listener’s question follows the exchange.

## Five review checks

**1. Question and answer**. Say the headline aloud, then read the first two object turns. The first must put a specific part of the headline at stake. The second has to answer, challenge, or complicate it. The ending should change what we would look for. Give closing research prompts their own field.

**2. Pair and evidence**. Identify the accession of each host and whether the reason for pairing is a shared tag, an artist’s comparison, or a documented historical relationship. Give each object at least one anchored detail where the material supports it. Read titles, tags, and documented facts at their actual level of certainty. A thin edge can pass as a deliberately skeptical scene if the dialogue makes that thinness the subject.

**3. Distinct voices and replies**. Read without names. Can you distinguish the speakers by what they want, what they notice, and how they speak? Underline a word or premise the second speaker picks up. For scripts longer than two lines, identify the line where a speaker concedes, adjusts, or leaves a sharper objection. Keep the same voice and delivery direction for an accession when its partner changes.

**4. Colloquial language and tone**. Try plain English aloud first. Add a trade word or light colloquial cue when it helps someone invite, interrupt, hedge, sell, or correct. One distinctive cue every few turns is enough. Use warmth, dry wit, occasional absurdity, and seriousness where they serve the objects. Wit in a political or violent scene should interrogate the rhetoric without making suffering the joke.

**5. Listener and room flow**. Name an object, phrase, material detail, or disagreement from the room and ask a new, answerable question. Try two different personas. Each should lead to a different thing to notice, compare, or look up. Keep present-day parallels here, labeled as interpretation. Make the question legible on a narrow phone and keep it outside the playback transcript.

## Language and evidence

| Level | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Object wording | A title, inscription or exact passage located on this object or in its checked record. Record the accession, transcription, locator and translation if needed. |
| Period-attested phrase | A dated source attests this phrase in a comparable place, setting and social register. Record speaker or audience, meaning, source, and exact page or timestamp. Its use in the script can be fictional. The room may treat it as a creative choice without attributing it to the object. |
| Authored language | A new line, joke or figure of speech for this artwork. Keep it easy to say and label it as authored in the review record. A phrase with incomplete evidence may be rewritten in plain English while verification continues. |

A French, Spanish, Italian, Dutch or other object can speak in a readable authored English translation register. Keep the original title in the record and give a translation locator for a borrowed expression. Use a performer’s delivery and the object’s function to distinguish voices. Treat nationality, class, disability, ethnicity and institutional role with specificity grounded in evidence. For example, the existing radio study identifies “dial twister” as a 1937 phrase with a candidate locator. Recheck the page and register before marking it attested, and use it as a crafted phrase if it suits the voice.

## The eleven listener perspectives

| Archetype | Persona and focus | How the listener sounds and what they ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Everyday readers | Family — care, access and ordinary routines | Use everyday words and practical consequences. Ask who gets to use it or what someone at home would notice. Leave household habits open unless a source documents them. |
| Everyday readers | Tourist — travel, thresholds and spectacle | Speak from an itinerary or viewpoint. Ask what the object invites a visitor to see and what the visit would take. Distinguish invitations from attended visits. |
| Everyday readers | Art Novice — a first look | Ask short, literal questions about a concrete feature. Give permission to look twice. Use curiosity as the register and avoid performing ignorance. |
| Everyday readers | Art Connoisseur — form, material and credit | Name a technique or contrast only when it helps someone see it. Ask how a material, composition or named maker changes the reading. |
| Everyday readers | Worker — making, upkeep and care | Use task verbs and specific trade language. Ask who made, moved, used or repaired it, and who carried the risk. Leave unions, workplaces and labor conditions to sources. |
| Archive & futures | Archivist — what can be established | Use short evidence verbs such as names, shows, lists and leaves open. Ask for one record that could settle a claim. Keep any authored intervention brief and fictional. |
| Archive & futures | Futurist — progress and recipients | Let the language of the promise carry the tone. Ask who was meant to benefit, which future looks inevitable, and what the object leaves outside it. |
| Archive & futures | Mourner — missing people and afterlives | Use measured, specific language. Ask what or who is absent and what survives for a reader to examine. Leave a person’s feelings to their own recorded account. |
| Critical experiments | Propagandist — how persuasion works | Notice pronouns, commands, implied rewards and thresholds. Ask who is being recruited. A pointed question can expose the pitch without repeating it as a recommendation. |
| Critical experiments | Counterfeit — plausible mistakes | State a tempting claim as a question, identify the missing evidence immediately, and offer a concrete means of checking it. Keep the correction beside the claim. |
| Critical experiments | The Museum — what can be shown responsibly | Use plain exhibition and collection language. Ask how a label or display would frame the uncertainty. Identify the persona as fictional and leave institutional endorsement to the institution. |

The perspective picker presents these groups and identities. “Open” describes a search mode.

## Review and release

Score each of the five checks 0, 1, or 2. A 0 needs revision, a 1 is plausible but needs editing or review, and a 2 passes on the evidence supplied. Scene and listener get separate decisions. Mark a script “Keep” when the writing works for a first review, “Polish” for a bounded edit, and “Revise” for an opening, reply or source-voice problem. Before releasing an edition, require a 2 on each applicable check and record its reviewer and decision. A read-aloud score and an editorial sign-off follow the written audit.

Stop promotion for unsupported historical claims, invented quotation, an unverified phrase presented as historical, a listener played as an object, uncertain rights, or a mistaken record identity. A strong total cannot clear a stop. A new evidence decision may open a research task while the script is worked on privately.

Work in batches of four. Start with the exact pair, the shared or proposed reason, the opening question and one defensible anchor per speaker. Read aloud once without speaker names, once with labels, then try two listeners and the room on a phone. Record one required change, an owner and a date for each unresolved item. Compare against another room with the same object to catch drift.

Before making audio, freeze the ordered object and any authored Archivist lines. Device reading and ElevenLabs use those exact words. The selected listener appears below the transcript. NotebookLM’s chosen pilot format is Debate, with research hosts, its own reviewed transcript, and a cleared source brief. A new script revision requires checking the linked performance and its cues. A revised listener question by itself keeps the existing performance eligible.

## Room worksheet

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Room and revision | Scene ID, the exact accessions and names, selected Git revision, reviewer and date. |
| Question and pairing reason | Opening question, source basis, two titles or details, closing source question kept separately. |
| Speech and register | First two lines and what one picks up from the other, the turn that changes the exchange, voice alias or direction by accession, and any phrase with evidence level and locator. |
| Listener test | Two named personas, two room-specific questions, and what each would make a visitor look at or look for. Record whether the prompts add anything the last spoken line hasn’t already said. |
| Decision | Five scores, source or rights holds, revision disposition, mobile and read-aloud result, next editor, and separate audio review where applicable. |

## Worked example

Making the Engine × Grind Safely asks “Who pays the price of progress?” The print notices the absence of workers in its title. The safety warning brings the risk down to one splinter and an eye. The print then acknowledges that it was talking about the image while the warning was talking about what the work might cost. A Worker listener can ask whose account could show who was expected to make grinding safer. An Archivist listener can ask which record would establish where the warning was actually displayed. Neither question requires inventing an injury or a shared workshop.
